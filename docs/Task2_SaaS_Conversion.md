# Task 2 — SaaS Conversion Analysis

*Eman Yahya · July 2026. Submitted as `Task 2 - SaaS Conversion Analysis - Eman Yahya.pdf`; this file mirrors that document verbatim.*

> **The question:** A SaaS platform focused on providing Family Office Intelligence to a targeted audience is only converting 3% of free accounts to paying users. The SaaS platform founders want to increase MRR. To that end, how would you improve this free trial to paid conversion rate?

## Where I started

I want to be honest about what I'm working with. The brief gives me one number: 3% of free accounts become paying customers. There is no cohort definition behind it, no picture of who signs up, and no description of what the free product actually includes. So this analysis is built on the structure of the problem rather than on your data, and I've tried to stay disciplined about which of my claims are facts, which are inferences I'd defend in a room with you, and which are bets I'd want to check before spending your money. Where I'm guessing, I say so.

Two things in the framing caught my attention before I got anywhere near solutions. The question calls them free *accounts*, and then a sentence later, a free *trial*. Those are not the same product. An expired-trial pool fails differently from a freemium pool, and a fix designed for one can make the other worse. If we were on a call, my first question would be which of the two you're actually running, and nothing in this document is safe from revision until I know the answer. I've written the rest of it assuming freemium, because "free accounts" that can sit at 3% suggests a pool that accumulates rather than expires, but that is an assumption and I'm flagging it as one.

The second thing is more important. Your goal is MRR. The question is about conversion. Those are related but they are not the same target, and confusing them is genuinely dangerous, because conversion is the easiest number in the funnel to manipulate without creating any revenue. Add friction at signup, the tourist traffic falls away, the rate doubles, and not one new dollar arrives — and teams have been known to celebrate exactly that. So throughout this document, conversion is the lever I'm working, but new MRR added per month is the number I'd actually manage to, and I'd ask you to hold me to that one.

And then there's the number itself. Three percent sounds bad. It sits, in fact, inside the normal 2–5% range for B2B freemium. That doesn't mean nothing is wrong. It means I'm not yet convinced the problem is where the question says it is. A low blended rate can be a conversion problem, or it can be a perfectly healthy conversion rate buried under a pile of signups who were never going to buy anything. Those two situations look identical from the outside and get fixed in completely different ways, which is why the first phase of my plan is diagnostic rather than corrective.

## The kind of business this is

Most freemium advice was written for software, and this isn't really a software business. It's a data business with software wrapped around it, and that changes the physics in two ways that I think explain more of the 3% than any onboarding flow does.

The first is that the value walks out the door. A free user who looks up thirty family offices and copies the principal's name into his own spreadsheet has not sampled your product. He has consumed it. In ordinary SaaS, the free tier demonstrates value that only continues if you pay; here, the free tier can quietly deliver the finished goods. This matters enormously because of who the highest-intent user is: a fund manager in the middle of a raise. For him the economics are absurd in your favor, since a single $10M allocation at a 2% management fee is worth $200k a year against a tool that costs a few thousand. His willingness to pay during the raise is close to unlimited. The moment the raise closes, it collapses to nothing. His job is episodic, and if the free tier lets him finish his prospect list without paying, he will, gratefully, and you will have subsidized your best customer at his moment of highest intent.

The second is that trust gets tested before value does. Watch what a new user does in a database product like this: almost invariably, the first search is for two or three family offices they already know. They're not researching. They're auditing you. If the AUM figure is five years stale, or the listed principal left in 2023, the tab closes and that person never comes back, and here's the uncomfortable part: your analytics will record it as a user who "viewed two profiles and churned," indistinguishable from a tourist. I'd call this the known-FO check, and I suspect a real share of your lost conversions dies right there, silently. I can't prove that from here. It's the kind of thing session recordings and a freshness audit would confirm or kill within two weeks, and I'd want both before believing my own theory.

There's one more piece of context I kept coming back to. FINTRX, Preqin, Dakota, With Intelligence: nobody else in this category runs freemium. The whole market sells demo-gated annual contracts, mostly in the $10k–50k range. When every incumbent refuses a model, that's not fashion, it's revealed preference, and the likeliest explanation is that they all concluded a free tier leaks the asset. I take that seriously. But I don't think the right response is to copy them, because their model abandons an entire segment: the solo GP or emerging manager who has acute need and no procurement path to a $15k contract. That segment is the strategic argument for keeping a free tier at all. The conclusion I'd draw is narrower than either extreme: freemium is defensible here only if the free tier is rebuilt to prove the data is good without handing over the part that's worth money. Which, concretely, means contact details and exports are never free. That single rule is the difference between a funnel and a leak.

## What I'd find out first

Before recommending anything structural, I'd spend two weeks and one engineer on evidence, because three of my four main hypotheses can be confirmed or destroyed cheaply, and I'd rather be wrong in week two than in month four.

The 3% needs to be rebuilt as a proper cohort number: of accounts created in a given month, what share paid within 30, 60, 90 days, and how long did they take? Time-to-convert matters more than the rate itself, because it tells you what's doing the converting. Payments at day two mean the gate is driving decisions; payments at day forty mean trust is. Then every signup, past and present, gets enriched by email domain so we can see who is actually in the pool. This costs almost nothing and it's the single highest-leverage query in this entire document. My bet, and it is a bet, is that the pool is majority tourists (students, researchers, competitors, the curious) converting near zero, while genuinely qualified accounts already convert somewhere near 8–10%. If that's right, the story flips from "our product doesn't convert" to "our funnel is full of people who were never going to buy," and those lead to opposite strategies. I'd also want the behavioral trace: what did the people who paid do in their first sessions that everyone else didn't, and how many profiles did they view before they hit a limit? That number, whatever it is, becomes load-bearing later. And I'd instrument two things I'd wager aren't tracked today, searches that return nothing and the age of the records people actually view, because those are the trust metrics and nothing else measures them.

Alongside the queries, fifteen or twenty phone calls: some who paid, some who left, and the group I'd most want to hear from, the heavy free users who never paid a cent. The data will tell us where the funnel leaks. Only the calls tell us why, and the second question on a call like that ("walk me through the last time you used it") is routinely worth more than a quarter of dashboards.

## What I think is actually happening

With the caveat that evidence beats my priors, here is how I'd rank the explanations, and why.

The most likely, to my mind, is the polluted pool I described above, simply because it's the default outcome of running an open free tier in a niche this interesting-sounding. Family offices attract curiosity far beyond the people who'd ever pay to reach them. It's also the cheapest hypothesis to test, which is why it goes first regardless of my confidence in it.

Close behind it is leakage, the episodic fundraiser finishing his job for free. The signature would be unmistakable in the data: accounts with intense usage over a few weeks that stops abruptly, no payment ever. If the enrichment shows qualified users who engage heavily and still don't convert, leakage becomes my lead theory and the gate redesign below becomes urgent rather than merely important.

Trust failure, the stale record killing the known-FO check, I hold at medium confidence only because I can't see it from here; nothing about it is unlikely, it's just invisible without instrumentation. I want to be careful not to fall in love with this theory, because it's the most intellectually satisfying of the four and satisfying theories deserve extra suspicion.

Then there's packaging. If one price shape is being sold to three different buyers, a solo raiser who needs a monthly plan for a six-month raise, a firm that needs seats and an invoice, an institution that needs API access, then some high-intent users are being blocked by the shape of the offer rather than its size. And finally, the one with the biggest dollar upside: if several people from the same fund's domain are sitting in the free pool, that is a five-figure enterprise deal parked in a $0 tier, and nobody has emailed them. The incumbents' entire business is built on precisely those buyers.

## What I'd do, in order

The sequencing matters more than the list, so let me explain the order rather than just assert it.

The first move costs nothing and could pay for everything else. While the instrumentation is being built, I'd pull a simple list of the warmest accounts, right domain, heavy usage, ideally multiple colleagues from the same firm, and have a founder personally email the top twenty. Not a campaign; twenty actual emails. This is the cheapest test of the enterprise thesis available, it produces revenue rather than consuming budget, and the replies are market research you cannot buy. My honest expectation is that two or three of those twenty turn into conversations worth five figures. If none do, that tells us something important too, and it cost a week of one founder's attention.

The structural work comes second, in months one and two, and it's the heart of the plan: rebuilding the free tier around proof instead of consumption. Full profile views get metered, with the cap set just below whatever the behavioral data says converters typically viewed before paying. That's a number we'll have from week two; I would not guess it, because a cap set too tight suppresses the trust-building the whole model depends on. Contact details and exports sit permanently behind payment, and exports carry a watermark. Onboarding stops being a tour and becomes the audit the user was going to run anyway: search three family offices you know, with the record's age and sourcing displayed plainly, because in this category showing your homework is the sales pitch. And the upgrade moment moves to the point of need, the click on a hidden contact detail, rather than a timer. One concern I'll name openly: exposing freshness dates cuts both ways, and if the audit shows the data can't survive daylight, then fixing data comes before any of this, because no growth mechanic outruns a stale database.

Only after that, months two and three, would I touch pricing, and I'd want the interview notes in hand first. The shape I expect to land on is three tiers (solo monthly, team annual with seats and CRM export, enterprise with an invoice and API), but I hold that loosely; I would avoid changing pricing until the calls tell us what the raise-window buyer and the firm buyer each actually need to say yes. Alongside it, the zero-result search log becomes the data team's roadmap, because in a data business, coverage decisions are growth decisions.

Further out there's a strategic move I'd start small: letting family offices claim and verify their own profiles. They are simultaneously your records and your supply side, and every verified profile compounds the one moat in this category that's actually durable, accuracy. It also improves your standing on the privacy question, which, given that this product traffics in personal contact data under GDPR's shadow, needs a lawyer's eyes regardless of anything in this document.

## How I'd know whether it's working

A short section, because most of what I have to say here is a refusal. At the volumes a business like this plausibly has, a clean A/B test on conversion itself is not honest: detecting a move from 3% to 4.5% needs roughly 2,600 users per arm, which is months per experiment. I won't pretend otherwise. Instead, experiments run on faster, higher-base-rate signals, activation and paywall-to-upgrade clicks, which resolve in four to six weeks, with conversion checked afterward on cohorts as confirmation. Pricing ships as before-and-after cohorts with the decision rule written down in advance, so we can't rationalize whatever happens. And every change carries the same two tripwires: if qualified signups or activation fall more than about 10–15% for two consecutive weeks, we revert first and debate second.

## What it might be worth

Treat this paragraph as arithmetic, not a forecast; every input gets replaced with your actuals a month in. Suppose a thousand signups a month at $250 average revenue per paying account, and suppose the pool splits 30% qualified converting around 8.7%, the rest near zero, which is exactly consistent with a 3% blend. If this program lifts qualified conversion to the neighborhood of 15% and mixes average revenue up toward $320 through team plans and those founder-led deals, new MRR per month goes from about $7.5k to $15–16k. Call it double the new-revenue run rate in roughly six months, for two engineers' time and some founder selling. If only the gate redesign lands, it's maybe twenty percent. Still worth it, and the instrumentation stays either way.

> **And the condition under which I'd abandon my own plan:** if, two quarters after the gate redesign, qualified conversion still sits under roughly 8% and the leakage signatures haven't faded, then freemium has lost its argument. At that point I'd stop defending it, move to a gated trial with a demo motion like everyone else in this category, and put the energy into sales. I'd rather commit to that trigger today than spend next year rationalizing sunk cost.

## The bottom line

I wouldn't start by optimizing conversion, because I'm not yet convinced conversion is the disease rather than the symptom. Based on the available information, I believe the 3% is a blended average hiding a qualified segment that converts adequately and a free tier that leaks the product to everyone else, and the plan above is sequenced so the cheap, reversible moves come first: see the funnel clearly, email the twenty warmest accounts, then rebuild the free tier as a trust engine instead of a free lunch. If my assumptions hold, conversion roughly doubles among the buyers who matter. But the number I'd put in front of you every month is new MRR, because that's the question you're actually asking.
